The Classical Problem by Backward Induction

The Classical Secretary Problem

A complete backward-induction solution

We derive the optimal stopping rule from the Bellman recursion

\[ V_n=\max\{U_n,W_n\}, \]

where \(U_n\) is the value of stopping at stage \(n\) and \(W_n\) is the value of continuing.

1. The model

There are \(N\) candidates with distinct absolute ranks

\[ 1,2,\ldots,N, \]

where absolute rank \(1\) denotes the best candidate.

The candidates arrive in a uniformly random order. After interviewing candidate \(n\), the decision maker must either:

  • accept the candidate and stop, or
  • reject the candidate permanently and continue.

The objective is to maximize the probability of selecting the candidate of absolute rank \(1\).

Only relative ranks are observed. The absolute ranks of the candidates are not known during the selection process.

2. Relative ranks

Let \(X_n\) denote the absolute rank of the candidate interviewed at time \(n\). Define the relative rank

\[ R_n = 1+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\mathbf{1}_{\{X_j<X_n\}}. \]

Thus, \(R_n\) is the rank of candidate \(n\) among the first \(n\) candidates.

In particular,

$$

R_n=1 $$

means that candidate \(n\) is better than every candidate observed previouslylkj

sebelumnya. Such a candidate is called a record.

Important

A candidate can be the overall best only if they are a record when observed. Therefore, it is never optimal to accept a candidate with \(R_n>1\).

3. Uniformity oflkj of relative ranks

Theorem

Under a uniformly random arrival order,

\[ R_n\sim \operatorname{Uniform}\{1,\ldots,n\}. \]

Moreover, the relative ranks

\[ R_1,R_2,\ldots,R_N \]

are mutually independent.

Proof

Every permutation

\[ (X_1,\ldots,X_N) \]

of the absolute ranks corresponds uniquely to a sequence of relative ranks satisfying

\[ R_n\in\{1,\ldots,n\}. \]

Conversely, every sequence

\[ (r_1,\ldots,r_N), \qquad r_n\in\{1,\ldots,n\}, \]

determines a unique permutation.

There are

\[ 1\cdot2\cdots N=N! \]

possible relative-rank sequences, exactly matching the \(N!\) permutations of the absolute ranks.

Since every permutation is equally likely, every admissible relative-rank sequence has probability \(1/N!\). Therefore,

\[ \Pr(R_1=r_1,\ldots,R_N=r_N) = \prod_{n=1}^{N}\frac1n. \]

It follows that the relative ranks are independent and

\[ \Pr(R_n=j)=\frac1n, \qquad j=1,\ldots,n. \]

In particular,

\[ \Pr(R_n=1)=\frac1n. \]

4. The reward from stopping

Suppose candidate \(n\) is a record. Define

\[ U_n = \Pr(X_n=1\mid R_n=1). \]

This is the conditional probability of winning if we stop at stage \(n\).

Now,

\[ \Pr(X_n=1)=\frac1N, \]

because the overall best candidate is equally likely to occupy any of the \(N\) positions. Also,

\[ \Pr(R_n=1)=\frac1n. \]

Since \(X_n=1\) implies \(R_n=1\),

\[ \Pr(X_n=1,R_n=1) = \Pr(X_n=1) = \frac1N. \]

Therefore,

\[ U_n = \frac{\Pr(X_n=1,R_n=1)} {\Pr(R_n=1)} = \frac{1/N}{1/n}. \]

Consequently,

\[ \boxed{U_n=\frac nN}. \]

Thus, when candidate \(n\) is a record, accepting that candidate succeeds with probability \(n/N\).

If \(R_n>1\), the candidate cannot be the overall best, so the stopping reward is zero.

5. The continuation value

Let \(W_n\) denote the optimal probability of eventually selecting the overall best after candidate \(n\) has been rejected.

When candidate \(n\) is a record, define

\[ V_n=\max\{U_n,W_n\}. \]

At stage \(n+1\), two cases are possible.

Case 1: Candidate \(n+1\) is a record

This occurs with probability

\[ \Pr(R_{n+1}=1)=\frac1{n+1}. \]

The decision maker may then stop or continue. Therefore, the optimal value is

\[ V_{n+1}. \]

Case 2: Candidate \(n+1\) is not a record

This occurs with probability

\[ \Pr(R_{n+1}>1)=\frac n{n+1}. \]

A nonrecord cannot be the overall best, so it must be rejected. The remaining value is

\[ W_{n+1}. \]

Combining the two cases gives the Bellman recursion

\[ \boxed{ W_n = \frac1{n+1}V_{n+1} + \frac n{n+1}W_{n+1} }. \]

Together with

\[ U_n=\frac nN \]

and

\[ V_n=\max\{U_n,W_n\}, \]

we obtain the backward-induction system

\[ \boxed{ \begin{aligned} U_n&=\frac nN,\\[4pt] V_n&=\max\{U_n,W_n\},\\[4pt] W_n&= \frac1{n+1}V_{n+1} + \frac n{n+1}W_{n+1}. \end{aligned} } \]

6. Terminal condition

At the final stage, there is no future candidate. Therefore,

\[ W_N=0. \]

If candidate \(N\) is a record, that candidate must be the overall best. Hence,

\[ U_N=1. \]

It follows that

\[ V_N=\max\{1,0\}=1. \]

These terminal values allow the recursion to be evaluated backward from \(n=N\) to \(n=1\).

7. The optimal decision rule

When candidate \(n\) is a record, compare

\[ U_n=\frac nN \]

with \(W_n\).

The optimal decision is

\[ \begin{cases} \text{stop}, & U_n\geq W_n,\\[4pt] \text{continue}, & U_n<W_n. \end{cases} \]

Equivalently,

\[ \boxed{ \text{accept candidate }n \iff R_n=1 \text{ and } \frac nN\geq W_n. } \]

The stopping value \(U_n=n/N\) increases with \(n\). Meanwhile, the value of waiting decreases as the end of the observation horizon approaches. Therefore, the stopping and continuation values cross at a threshold.

Consequently, there is an integer \(r\) such that:

  • record candidates in positions \(1,\ldots,r\) are rejected;
  • the first record after position \(r\) is accepted.

The threshold rule is therefore derived from backward induction; it is not assumed in advance.

8. Complete example: \(N=10\)

We now solve the problem recursively for ten candidates.

The terminal values are

\[ W_{10}=0, \qquad U_{10}=1, \qquad V_{10}=1. \]

At stage \(9\),

\[ W_9 = \frac1{10}V_{10} + \frac9{10}W_{10} = \frac1{10}. \]

Since

\[ U_9=\frac9{10}>\frac1{10}=W_9, \]

we stop at a record in position \(9\).

Continuing backward gives the following table.

The first stage at which stopping becomes optimal is \(n=4\). Therefore, the optimal policy is

\[ \boxed{ \text{reject candidates }1,2,3 \text{ and accept the first later record.} } \]

Thus, the optimal rejection threshold is

\[ \boxed{r^\star=3}. \]

The optimal success probability is

\[ \boxed{ V_1 = \frac{3349}{8400} \approx 0.3986904762. } \]

9. Recovering the exact threshold formula

Suppose the first \(r\) candidates have been rejected. Under the threshold strategy, the overall best is selected if:

  1. the overall best occurs at some position \(k>r\); and
  2. the best among the first \(k-1\) candidates occurs among the first \(r\).

For a fixed \(k>r\),

\[ \Pr(X_k=1)=\frac1N. \]

Conditional on the overall best appearing at position \(k\), the best candidate among positions \(1,\ldots,k-1\) is equally likely to occupy any of those \(k-1\) positions. Therefore,

\[ \Pr\left( \text{best of the first }k-1 \text{ lies among the first }r \right) = \frac r{k-1}. \]

Hence,

\[ \Pr(\text{win with the best at }k) = \frac1N\frac r{k-1}. \]

Summing over \(k=r+1,\ldots,N\) gives

\[ \boxed{ P_N(r) = \frac rN \sum_{k=r+1}^{N}\frac1{k-1} }. \]

Equivalently,

\[ \boxed{ P_N(r) = \frac rN \sum_{j=r}^{N-1}\frac1j }. \]

This is precisely the continuation value \(W_r\) obtained after rejecting the first \(r\) candidates:

\[ \boxed{W_r=P_N(r)}. \]

10. Characterization of the optimal threshold

The optimal threshold is the integer \(r^\star\) for which it is optimal to continue at \(r^\star\) but stop at the next record.

Continuing at stage \(r\) requires

\[ W_r\geq U_r. \]

Using

\[ W_r = \frac rN\sum_{j=r}^{N-1}\frac1j \]

and

\[ U_r=\frac rN, \]

this condition becomes

\[ \sum_{j=r}^{N-1}\frac1j\geq 1. \]

Stopping at a record in position \(r+1\) requires

\[ U_{r+1}\geq W_{r+1}, \]

which is equivalent to

\[ \sum_{j=r+1}^{N-1}\frac1j\leq 1. \]

Therefore, the optimal threshold is characterized by

\[ \boxed{ \sum_{j=r^\star+1}^{N-1}\frac1j \leq 1 \leq \sum_{j=r^\star}^{N-1}\frac1j }. \]

Equivalently,

\[ \boxed{ r^\star = \min\left\{ r: \sum_{j=r+1}^{N-1}\frac1j\leq 1 \right\}. } \]

For \(N=10\),

\[ \sum_{j=4}^{9}\frac1j<1 \]

but

\[ \sum_{j=3}^{9}\frac1j>1. \]

Therefore,

\[ r^\star=3. \]

11. The asymptotic \(1/e\) rule

For large \(N\), the harmonic sum satisfies

\[ \sum_{j=r}^{N-1}\frac1j \approx \int_r^N\frac{dx}{x} = \log\left(\frac Nr\right). \]

At the optimal threshold, this quantity is approximately \(1\). Thus,

\[ \log\left(\frac{N}{r^\star}\right)\approx 1. \]

Exponentiating gives

\[ \frac{N}{r^\star}\approx e, \]

and therefore

\[ \boxed{ \frac{r^\star}{N}\longrightarrow\frac1e }. \]

The optimal success probability satisfies

\[ P_N(r^\star) = \frac{r^\star}{N} \sum_{j=r^\star}^{N-1}\frac1j. \]

Since

\[ \frac{r^\star}{N}\longrightarrow\frac1e \]

and the harmonic sum converges to \(1\),

\[ \boxed{ P_N(r^\star)\longrightarrow\frac1e }. \]

Thus, for a large number of candidates, the optimal strategy is approximately:

  1. reject the first \(N/e\) candidates;
  2. accept the first subsequent record.

Both the optimal rejection proportion and the limiting success probability approach

\[ \boxed{\frac1e\approx 0.367879}. \]

12. Final result

TipOptimal classical secretary rule

For \(N\) candidates arriving in uniformly random order:

  1. Determine the threshold \(r^\star\) from

    \[ \sum_{j=r^\star+1}^{N-1}\frac1j \leq 1 \leq \sum_{j=r^\star}^{N-1}\frac1j. \]

  2. Reject the first \(r^\star\) candidates.

  3. Accept the first later candidate whose relative rank is \(1\).

The rule follows from the recursion

\[ V_n=\max\{U_n,W_n\}, \]

where

\[ U_n=\frac nN \]

and

\[ W_n = \frac1{n+1}V_{n+1} + \frac n{n+1}W_{n+1}. \]

As \(N\to\infty\),

\[ \frac{r^\star}{N}\longrightarrow\frac1e, \qquad P_N(r^\star)\longrightarrow\frac1e. \]

13. The backward-induction theorem

The preceding solution is an application of the general finite-horizon optimal stopping theorem (Chow, Robbins, and Siegmund 1971; Ferguson 2010; Shiryaev 2007).

Theorem

Let

\[ (\Omega,\mathcal F,\{\mathcal F_n\}_{n=1}^{N},\Pr) \]

be a filtered probability space. Let

\[ U_1,U_2,\ldots,U_N \]

be an integrable adapted reward process, where \(U_n\) is the reward obtained by stopping at time \(n\).

For each \(n\), let \(\mathcal T_n\) denote the set of stopping times taking values in

\[ \{n,n+1,\ldots,N\}. \]

Define the value process by

\[ V_n = \operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{\tau\in\mathcal T_n} \operatorname E[U_\tau\mid\mathcal F_n]. \]

Then

\[ V_N=U_N \]

and, for \(n=N-1,\ldots,1\),

\[ \boxed{ V_n = \max\left\{ U_n, \operatorname E[V_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n] \right\}. } \]

If the continuation value is defined by

\[ W_n = \operatorname E[V_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n], \]

then the recursion becomes

\[ \boxed{V_n=\max\{U_n,W_n\}}. \]

Moreover, subject to forced stopping at time \(N\), the stopping time

\[ \boxed{ \tau^\star = \inf\{n:U_n\geq W_n\} } \]

is optimal.

Proof

At time \(N\), stopping is mandatory. Therefore,

\[ V_N=U_N. \]

Suppose that \(V_{n+1}\) is the optimal value from time \(n+1\) onward.

At time \(n\), the decision maker has two possible actions:

  1. stop immediately and receive \(U_n\);

  2. continue and receive the conditional expected optimal future value

    \[ W_n = \operatorname E[V_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]. \]

An optimal decision selects the action with the larger value. Hence,

\[ V_n=\max\{U_n,W_n\}. \]

Starting from \(V_N=U_N\) and repeating this argument for

\[ n=N-1,N-2,\ldots,1 \]

determines the entire value process by backward induction.

At every time \(n\), stopping when \(U_n\geq W_n\) selects an action attaining the value \(V_n\). Therefore, the first time at which this inequality holds is an optimal stopping time:

\[ \tau^\star = \inf\{n:U_n\geq W_n\}. \]

This proves the result. \(\square\)

Application to the classical secretary problem

In the classical secretary problem, a nonrecord candidate cannot be the overall best and therefore has stopping value zero.

If candidate \(n\) is a record, the immediate stopping value is

\[ U_n=\frac nN. \]

The continuation value is

\[ W_n = \operatorname E[V_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]. \]

Because

\[ \Pr(R_{n+1}=1)=\frac1{n+1}, \]

we obtain

\[ W_n = \frac1{n+1}V_{n+1} + \frac n{n+1}W_{n+1}. \]

Consequently, the secretary-problem recursion

\[ V_n=\max\{U_n,W_n\} \]

is a direct specialization of the finite-horizon backward-induction theorem.

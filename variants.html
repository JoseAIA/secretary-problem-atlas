Variants & Extensions of the Secretary Problem

The classical secretary problem is only the starting point.

Changing the information available, the objective, the number of choices, or the horizon leads to genuinely different optimal-stopping problems.

The classical benchmark

There are \(n\) candidates whose absolute ranks form a uniformly random permutation of

\[ 1,2,\ldots,n, \]

with rank \(1\) best.

Candidates are observed sequentially. At time \(k\), only the candidate’s relative rank among the first \(k\) observations is known. Decisions are irrevocable, and only one candidate may be selected.

The objective is

\[ \max_{\tau}\Pr(R_\tau=1). \]

For a threshold \(r\), reject the first \(r\) candidates and then accept the first subsequent record. Its success probability is

\[ P_n(r) = \frac{r}{n} \sum_{j=r+1}^{n}\frac{1}{j-1}. \]

As \(n\to\infty\), the optimal threshold satisfies

\[ \frac{r_n}{n}\to \frac1e, \]

and the optimal success probability also converges to \(1/e\).

The extensions below can be organized by asking which part of this benchmark is changed.

1. Full-information secretary problem

Problem

Instead of seeing only relative ranks, suppose the actual observations

\[ X_1,\ldots,X_n \]

are revealed sequentially, typically as independent observations from a known continuous distribution \(F\).

The objective remains

\[ \Pr\!\left(X_\tau=\max_{1\le j\le n}X_j\right). \]

What changes?

The numerical value of the current observation contains information about how likely future observations are to exceed it.

The optimal decision therefore depends not only on whether the current observation is a record, but also on how large that record is.

Optimal-strategy structure

Using the probability-integral transform,

\[ U_i=F(X_i), \]

the problem can be reduced to independent \(U(0,1)\) observations.

The optimal rule is described by time-dependent critical values: a record is accepted only when its value is sufficiently large relative to the number of observations remaining.

Connection to the classical model

Both problems seek the overall maximum.

The difference is informational:

  • no information: only relative ranks are observed;
  • full information: the actual values are observed.

Gilbert and Mosteller developed the foundational analysis of both settings.

2. The postdoc problem: selecting the second best

Problem

Change the reward from selecting absolute rank \(1\) to selecting absolute rank \(2\):

\[ \max_{\tau}\Pr(R_\tau=2). \]

The arrival order is still uniformly random, decisions remain irrevocable, and only relative ranks are observed.

What changes?

The current record is no longer the only potentially relevant candidate. A candidate with current relative rank \(2\) may also be strategically important.

Thus a small change in the terminal reward changes the state space of the stopping problem.

Connection to the classical model

The information structure is unchanged. Only the reward changes:

\[ \mathbf 1_{\{R_\tau=1\}} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \mathbf 1_{\{R_\tau=2\}}. \]

This makes the postdoc problem a particularly useful example of how the optimal rule can change when the target rank changes.

3. Multiple-choice and dowry problems

Problem

Suppose the decision maker is allowed more than one selection.

With \(m\) available choices, the decision maker chooses stopping times

\[ \tau_1<\tau_2<\cdots<\tau_m. \]

The success event depends on the particular model—for example, whether one of the selected observations is the overall best, or whether several top ranks must be captured.

Sakaguchi’s contribution

Sakaguchi’s work is an important early part of this branch of the literature.

His 1978 paper

Dowry Problems and OLA Policies

studied secretary-type stopping rules in a dowry formulation and belongs to the early development of multiple-stopping extensions.

Sakaguchi also studied generalized secretary models with uncertain employment, showing that changing the selection mechanism itself can produce new optimal-stopping structures.

Tamaki’s double-choice problems

Tamaki subsequently analyzed explicit two-choice secretary problems.

In one formulation, the decision maker is allowed two choices and seeks to select either the best or the second-best candidate. In another, the objective is to recognize both the maximum and the second maximum.

These problems illustrate why multiple-choice secretary models require more state information than the classical one-choice problem.

Dynamic-programming structure

If \(j\) selections remain at time \(k\), the state must record both the information observed so far and the remaining number of choices.

Schematically,

\[ V_{k,j}(s) = \max\{ \text{value of selecting now}, \text{value of continuing} \}. \]

Selecting now changes the state from \(j\) remaining choices to \(j-1\).

Connection to the classical model

The classical secretary problem is recovered when

\[ m=1. \]

Thus multiple-choice models enlarge the stopping problem by adding a resource variable: the number of selections still available.

4. Unknown or random horizon

Problem

The classical model assumes that the total number \(n\) of observations is known.

Suppose instead that the number of observations is a random variable

\[ N. \]

The distribution of \(N\) may be known even though its realized value is not.

What changes?

With a fixed horizon, the decision maker knows exactly how many observations remain.

With a random horizon, the continuation value must also account for the probability that the sequence ends.

This produces stopping rules that depend on both relative rank information and uncertainty about the remaining horizon.

Connection to the \(1/e\) phenomenon

Random-horizon best-choice models are one of the settings in which \(1/e\)-type laws appear beyond the classical fixed-\(n\) problem.

5. Last-success problems and the odds theorem

Problem

Let

\[ I_1,\ldots,I_n \]

be independent Bernoulli random variables with

\[ \Pr(I_k=1)=p_k. \]

The objective is to stop on the last success.

Define

\[ q_k=1-p_k, \qquad r_k=\frac{p_k}{q_k}. \]

Bruss’s odds theorem provides an elegant optimal rule based on summing the odds backward from the end of the sequence.

Connection to secretary problems

The odds theorem is not the classical secretary problem itself.

It belongs to the same broader optimal-stopping family: the decision maker must act without knowing whether a future event will supersede the current one.

6. Applicant-side secretary problems

Problem

The classical problem is posed from the employer’s perspective:

When should the employer stop?

Glass reversed the perspective.

Suppose the employer follows a known stopping rule and an applicant may choose an interview position \(i\).

The applicant seeks to maximize

\[ \Pr(\text{selected}\mid\text{position }i). \]

What changes?

The decision variable is no longer a stopping time.

Instead,

\[ \tau \quad\longrightarrow\quad i. \]

The employer’s stopping rule becomes part of the environment faced by the applicant.

Known self-rank

If the applicant knows their own absolute rank, the best position can depend strongly on that rank.

This produces a strategic positioning problem built on top of a classical secretary stopping rule.

7. Uncertain self-rank

Suppose the applicant does not know their absolute rank.

Let

\[ R\in\{1,\ldots,n\} \]

be random with a prior distribution. For example, a truncated geometric prior is

\[ \Pr(R=j) = \frac{(1-q)q^{j-1}}{1-q^n}, \qquad 0<q<1. \]

The applicant’s value from position \(i\) becomes

\[ V(i) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} \Pr(R=j) \Pr(\text{selected}\mid R=j,\text{position }i). \]

This turns applicant positioning into a Bayesian decision problem.

A useful way to generate new secretary problems is therefore to modify one or more components of the classical model:

Information + Objective + Choice structure + Horizon

Each modification can produce a genuinely different optimal-stopping problem.

Classical Secretary Problem: Proof and the 37% Rule

Derive the classical secretary problem’s exact success probability, optimal finite-n threshold, and 1/e rule, with an interactive simulation and sources.

The Classical Secretary Problem

Why should you reject roughly the first 37% of candidates before choosing? The secretary problem is a classical optimal stopping problem: decisions are sequential, rejections are final, and success means selecting the single best candidate.

The exact finite-sample solution and its asymptotic 37% rule are derived below; you can also experiment with the rule.

Model and assumptions

There are \(n\ge 2\) candidates, ranked absolutely from \(1\) (best) to \(n\) (worst). The classical no-information model assumes:

  1. The candidates arrive in a uniformly random order (all \(n!\) orders are equally likely).
  2. You observe only each candidate’s relative rank among those seen so far, not their absolute rank.
  3. You may accept at most one candidate. Every rejection is irrevocable.
  4. Your objective is to maximize the probability of choosing the single overall best candidate.

A candidate is a record if they are better than all candidates seen before. Since a nonrecord cannot be the overall best, there is no benefit to selecting one when the objective is the overall best.

The threshold strategy

Fix an integer \(r\) with \(0\le r<n\):

  1. Reject the first \(r\) candidates, observing their relative ranks.
  2. Starting at position \(r+1\), accept the first record (a candidate better than every earlier candidate).
  3. If there is no subsequent record, no candidate is selected and the attempt fails.

For \(r=0\), the rule accepts the first candidate immediately. This convention is used in the interactive simulation below.

The classical theorem establishes that a threshold rule is optimal among all stopping rules based on relative ranks, not merely the best rule within a preselected class of thresholds. A backward-induction proof of this optimal-policy structure is provided on the Backward Induction page.

Exact success probability: a finite-\(n\) derivation

Let \(P_n(r)\) denote the probability that the threshold-\(r\) strategy selects absolute rank \(1\).

Proposition. For \(1\le r<n\),

\[ \boxed{ P_n(r)=\frac{r}{n}\sum_{k=r+1}^{n}\frac{1}{k-1} =\frac{r}{n}\left(H_{n-1}-H_{r-1}\right), } \]

where \(H_m=\sum_{j=1}^m 1/j\) for \(m\ge1\) and \(H_0=0\). For \(r=0\), \(P_n(0)=1/n\).

Proof. Write \(K\) for the arrival position of the overall best candidate. Uniform random order gives

\[ \Pr(K=k)=\frac1n,\qquad k=1,\ldots,n. \]

If \(K\le r\), that candidate is rejected, so success is impossible. If \(K=k>r\), the strategy selects the overall best precisely when the best candidate among the first \(k-1\) arrivals occurred during the initial \(r\) positions. Otherwise, a record after position \(r\) would have triggered selection earlier.

Conditionally on \(K=k\), each of the first \(k-1\) positions is equally likely to hold the best among those first \(k-1\) candidates. Thus

\[ \Pr(\text{success}\mid K=k)=\frac{r}{k-1},\qquad k>r. \]

Summing over the possible positions of the overall best gives

\[ P_n(r) =\sum_{k=r+1}^{n}\Pr(K=k)\Pr(\text{success}\mid K=k) =\frac{r}{n}\sum_{k=r+1}^{n}\frac{1}{k-1}. \]

If \(r=0\), the first candidate is accepted, which is the overall best with probability \(1/n\). \(\square\)

Which threshold is optimal for a given \(n\)?

Finite-\(n\) criterion. For \(1\le r<n-1\),

\[ \boxed{ P_n(r+1)-P_n(r) =\frac1n\left(\sum_{j=r+1}^{n-1}\frac1j-1\right). } \]

To see this, subtract the two formulas:

\[ \begin{aligned} nP_n(r+1)&=(r+1)\sum_{j=r+1}^{n-1}\frac1j,\\ nP_n(r)&=1+r\sum_{j=r+1}^{n-1}\frac1j. \end{aligned} \]

The sum in the difference decreases strictly as \(r\) increases. Consequently the success probabilities increase until the displayed sum first becomes at most \(1\), then decrease. For \(n\ge3\), one optimal threshold is therefore

\[ \boxed{ r_n^\star=\min\left\{r\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}: \sum_{j=r+1}^{n-1}\frac1j\le1\right\}. } \]

An empty sum is \(0\). If the sum equals \(1\) at the transition, the adjacent threshold \(r_n^\star+1\) is also optimal. For \(n=2\), both \(r=0\) and \(r=1\) have success probability \(1/2\).

For example, with \(n=10\), the optimal threshold is \(r^\star=3\), and

$ P_{10}(3)=(+++). $$

This exact threshold need not equal the integer obtained by simply rounding \(n/e\).

Why the 37% rule appears

Suppose \(n\) grows and \(r/n\to x\in(0,1)\). The harmonic sum satisfies

\[ \sum_{j=r}^{n-1}\frac1j =\log\left(\frac nr\right)+o(1), \]

so

\[ P_n(r)=\frac rn\sum_{j=r}^{n-1}\frac1j \longrightarrow -x\log x. \]

Differentiating yields

\[ \frac{d}{dx}[-x\log x]=-\log x-1. \]

This vanishes at \(x=e^{-1}\), where the second derivative \(-1/x\) is negative. The finite-\(n\) optimality criterion above also implies that \(r_n^\star/n\to 1/e\). Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \frac{r_n^\star}{n}\longrightarrow\frac1e, \qquad P_n(r_n^\star)\longrightarrow\frac1e \approx 0.367879. } \]

Interpretation: for a large pool, reject about 36.8% of candidates, then accept the next record. The limiting probability of choosing the best is also about 36.8%. These are two distinct quantities that happen to share the same limit; for a fixed finite \(n\), they generally differ.
